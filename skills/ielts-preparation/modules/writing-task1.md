# IELTS Writing Task 1 Coach

Task 1 is the shorter of the two Writing tasks: roughly 20 of the 60 minutes, at least 150 words, and one third of the Writing band (Task 2 counts double).

- **Academic:** a factual report on one or more visuals. The writer picks out what matters, states the big picture, and backs it with figures. No opinions, no causes the visual does not show, no conclusion that recommends anything.
- **General Training:** a letter that answers a situation and three bullet points. See [General Training letters](#general-training-letters).

## Load this module when

- The learner brings a Task 1 prompt (chart, graph, table, map, process, or letter task) and wants help planning, drafting, or a critique.
- The learner wants a band estimate, or resubmits a revised draft.
- The learner asks about overviews, choosing features, or the language of numbers, change, and comparison.

Switch to `modules/writing-task2.md` for essays and to `modules/speaking.md` for Speaking.

---

## How Task 1 is marked

Four criteria, equal weight. What each one rewards in this task:

| Criterion | Academic report | GT letter |
|---|---|---|
| Task Achievement (TA) | A clear overview; the right features chosen and supported with accurate figures; report format | Purpose obvious; all three bullets dealt with and developed; tone suits the reader throughout |
| Coherence and Cohesion (CC) | Logical grouping, one focus per paragraph, linking and referencing that guide the reader without sounding mechanical | Same, plus a sensible letter shape |
| Lexical Resource (LR) | Precise words for change, size, and proportion; varied without forced synonyms; accurate spelling and word form | Words and set phrases at the right level of formality |
| Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA) | A mix of simple and complex sentences, controlled tense and voice, correct punctuation | Same |

Hard limits that hold a TA score down whatever else is good: no overview (Academic), a missing or barely touched bullet (GT), purpose unclear (GT), fewer than 150 words, figures misread, or a memorised script that ignores the prompt.

For band-by-band detail use `../references/band-descriptors.md` (section "Writing Task 1"), and for grammar the section "Grammatical Range and Accuracy (Writing and Speaking)". When `~/.ielts/official/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf` exists, read it for the exact official wording before settling a band.

## Academic workflow

### Step 1 — Read the visual before writing anything

Spend two or three minutes on this. Establish:

- **Type and time frame.** Is there change over time (line graph, bar chart across years, before/after maps) or a single moment (pie, table for one year, a process)? This decides the tense: past for past dates, present for an undated process, future forms (*is projected to*, *is expected to*) for forecasts.
- **Units.** Percentages, millions, tonnes, dollars. Misreading the unit is a factual error.
- **The story.** What would you tell a colleague in one breath? That sentence is the seed of the overview.

What to look for, by visual:

| Visual | Look for | Usual body split |
|---|---|---|
| Line graph | Overall direction of each line, starting and finishing points, highest and lowest points, where lines cross | Rising lines vs falling lines, or first period vs second period |
| Bar chart | Ranking, the biggest and smallest bars, the widest gaps, any category that breaks the pattern | Leading categories vs the rest, or one group of bars vs another |
| Pie chart(s) | Dominant and minor shares; with several pies, which shares grew or shrank | Large shares vs small shares, or changes between pies |
| Table | Extremes in each row and column, totals, outliers; never narrate cell by cell | By row group or by column group |
| Map (before/after) | What was built, removed, or converted; the general direction of change (more housing, less farmland) | By area of the map or by type of change |
| Process | Number of stages, start and end product, whether it is a cycle or a line, natural or man-made | First half of the stages vs second half |
| Two different visuals | One headline from each and any link between them | One paragraph per visual |

### Step 2 — Select the features

The examiner rewards choosing, not copying. Rank everything you noticed and keep about four to six features: the extremes, the overall direction, the most striking comparison, and any exception. Figures that support none of these stay out.

Selection checks:
- Could a reader sketch the chart roughly from the report? If not, a key feature is missing.
- Is every number there to prove a point? If a figure proves nothing, cut it.
- Are similar items grouped (*the three Asian cities all*) rather than listed one by one?

### Step 3 — Plan and draft

**Shape (four paragraphs):**

1. **Opening** — one sentence saying what the visual shows, where, when, and in what unit, in your own words. Change the grammar of the prompt, not only single words.
2. **Overview** — two general statements about the most important patterns. No figures. This paragraph carries more TA weight than any other; it can also sit at the end, but putting it second keeps the writer honest.
3. **Body A** and 4. **Body B** — the selected details with figures, grouped by the split from Step 1, comparing as you go.

For a process: opening, overview (how many stages, start and end), then the stages in order across two paragraphs. For maps: opening, overview (the main kinds of change), then changes by area or by type.

**Timing (about 20 minutes):** 3 reading and selecting, 2 planning, 12 writing, 3 checking figures, tense, and word count. Aim for 160 to 190 words; going much longer usually means listing data.

**Illustrative snippets** (invented data, for demonstration only):

> *Prompt idea:* the percentage of commuters who cycled to work in three cities, 2000 to 2020.
>
> *Opening:* The line graph compares the share of workers who travelled to work by bicycle in Amsterdam, Lyon, and Leeds over a twenty-year period.
>
> *Overview:* Cycling became more common in all three cities, but Amsterdam stayed far ahead throughout, while Leeds grew most slowly and slipped from second place to last.
>
> *Body sentence:* Lyon's figure doubled from 6% to 12%, overtaking Leeds in 2008 and widening the gap after that.

When drafting with the learner, have them write the overview first; it forces the selection.

**When critiquing**, work in this order: is there an overview and is it truly general; were the right features chosen; do the figures match the visual; is there any opinion or unsupported explanation; then CC, LR, GRA.

### Data language

**Change over time.** Two patterns, so sentences do not all look alike:
- verb + adverb: *Sales rose sharply.* / *The figure dipped slightly.*
- noun + adjective: *There was a sharp rise in sales.* / *Sales saw a slight dip.*

| Meaning | Verbs | Nouns |
|---|---|---|
| Up | rise, increase, grow, climb, jump, surge | a rise, an increase, growth, a jump |
| Down | fall, decrease, decline, drop, plunge | a fall, a decrease, a decline, a drop |
| No change | remain stable, level off, plateau | a plateau, a period of stability |
| Up and down | fluctuate | fluctuations |
| Highest / lowest | peak at, reach a high of, hit a low of, bottom out at | a peak, a low point |

Degree: *slightly, marginally, gradually, steadily, considerably, sharply, dramatically*. Use the strong words only when the data justifies them.

**Precision traps.** *rose **to** 40%* (the new level) vs *rose **by** 40%* (the size of the change); *an increase **in** exports* (what changed) vs *an increase **of** 5%* (how much); *percent* after a number, *percentage* as a noun (*the percentage of*); *the number of* for countable things, *the amount of* for uncountable ones.

**Approximation and proportion.** *just under / just over / roughly / almost*; *a quarter, nearly a third, the majority, a small minority*; *one in five households*.

**Comparison.** *X was twice as high as Y*; *X was three times larger than Y*; *X overtook Y in 2010*; *the gap between X and Y narrowed*; *the highest / the lowest proportion*; *whereas, while, by contrast*; *compared with*.

**Process.** Present simple passive for man-made steps (*The beans are roasted and then ground*), active for natural ones (*Water evaporates*). Sequence with *first, next, after this, once X has been done, at the final stage*.

**Maps.** Passives for change (*The factory was demolished and replaced by flats*; *A car park has been built*). Location: *in the north-east, next to, opposite, along the river, on the site of*.

**Indonesian-L1 checks** (look for these in every draft):
- **Tense marking.** Indonesian verbs do not change for time, so check that past dates get past verbs (*In 2005 the figure **rose***, not *rise*), and that present perfect is not used with a finished year.
- **Plurals.** Nouns after numbers and quantity words need *-s* (*five countries, many households*), but *million / thousand* stay singular after a number (*three million people*).
- **Articles.** *The* before superlatives and specific measures (*the highest figure, the proportion of*), none before general plurals (*Cars were the most popular*).
- **Passive with *be*.** The Indonesian *di-* passive has no auxiliary; learners write *The sugar heated*. Insist on *is heated*.
- **Subject–verb agreement** with long subjects: *The number of students **was***, not *were*.

### Step 4 — Score and feedback

1. **Learner predicts first.** Before showing any score, ask: "What band do you think you got for TA, CC, LR, GRA?" (one line). Skip only if the user refuses.
2. **Score with the calibration protocol** in `../references/calibration.md`: gates and caps first (word count, overview, GT bullets/tone), then per criterion quote evidence, pick the best-fit descriptor, and check "why not one higher / one lower" against `../references/band-descriptors.md` (section "Writing Task 1"; exact wording in `~/.ielts/official/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf` when it exists); compare with the nearest official anchor. Run `python3 scripts/ielts.py status` once per session and, if it flags a coach calibration offset (ADJUST) for a W1 criterion, correct for it and say so.
3. Report:

```
Task Achievement:     X / 9   (you predicted Y)
Coherence & Cohesion: X / 9   (you predicted Y)
Lexical Resource:     X / 9   (you predicted Y)
Grammatical Range:    X / 9   (you predicted Y)
Estimated overall:    X.X / 9   — estimate, range X.X–X.X if evidence is mixed
```

Wherever a criterion sits under 7, name two or three concrete changes, each shown on a sentence the learner actually wrote. If the prediction was off by ≥1 band on a criterion, name the descriptor feature they misjudged — that is the self-editing skill being trained.

4. **Tag and log.** Tag each problem with a skill id from `../references/skill-map.json` (`w1.overview`, `w1.selection`, `w1.data-language`, `w1.gt-letter`, `x.register`, `x.cohesion`, `x.collocation`, grammar codes such as `ART`, `TENSE`, …), then:

```
python3 scripts/ielts.py log --module writing-task1 --task "line graph: car use 1990-2020" \
  --predicted W1.TA=6.5 W1.CC=6.5 W1.LR=6 W1.GRA=6 \
  --score W1.TA=6 W1.CC=6.5 W1.LR=6 W1.GRA=5.5 \
  --tag w1.overview w1.data-language:2 TENSE:3 x.collocation
```

Add systematic grammar errors to the ledger (`error add --skill <CODE> --example ...`), LR fixes to the vocabulary deck (`vocab add ... --source writing`), and record deck terms used correctly (`vocab used "<term>"`). Close with **one** drill from `../references/drills.md` for the top tag (e.g. `overview-sprint`).

### Step 5 — Revise and re-score (when the user resubmits)

Feedback ends with the learner rewriting, not with you rewriting.

1. **Try-fix gate** — list 3–5 weak sentences or missing functions (missing overview, unextended bullet point, inaccurate figure). Do not give the fix until the learner has tried, unless they ask for immediate answers.
2. **One criterion per round** — pick the single criterion furthest below target and work only on that. When several are tied, start with TA, then CC, then LR and GRA together. Keep the learner's ideas and data selection; don't swap in a model answer.
3. **Guided edit** — after their attempt, show a micro-edit table `Original | Edit | Why | Tag` with tags `[keep]` `[replace]` `[+add]` `[-trim]`. Edit only what's needed; give a full rewrite only on request.
4. **Re-score against the previous draft** — compare with the earlier draft in the conversation: report per-criterion movement (`TA 6 → 6.5`), which errors returned, which are fixed, and the next criterion to target.
5. **Micro-practice** — up to 3 short exercises on the most frequent problem; the learner answers first, then show the key.

## General Training letters

The prompt describes a situation and usually gives three bullet points. Same four criteria; Task Achievement here judges purpose, bullet coverage, and tone (no overview is needed).

### Task gates and band caps

- **Purpose** is clear in the opening sentence or two. An unclear purpose keeps TA at 5 or below.
- **Every bullet point is covered and extended** with relevant detail. A missing bullet point means TA cannot reach 6 ("does not cover all bullet points" is band 4 territory); a bullet answered in one bare sentence keeps TA at 5–6.
- **Tone matches the relationship and stays consistent.** Band 7 requires "tone consistent and appropriate"; inconsistent tone caps TA at 6. A formal letter that drifts into "I wanna" or "How's it going?" also hurts LR.
- **Format:** greeting and closing that match the relationship; paragraphs; no addresses or date needed.
- At least 150 words. No upper limit, but an over-long backstory usually costs CC.

### Register — decided by who you are writing to

| Register | Reader | Greeting | Closing | Language |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal | Unknown person or organisation; complaints, applications | `Dear Sir or Madam,` / `Dear Mr Smith,` | `Yours faithfully,` (unnamed) / `Yours sincerely,` (named) | No contractions, no slang, no exclamation marks; softened requests with *would / could* |
| Semi-formal | Someone you know but not closely (landlord, colleague, neighbour, manager) | `Dear Mr/Ms {surname},` | `Kind regards,` / `Yours sincerely,` | Polite, a little more natural; limited contractions |
| Informal | Friend or family | `Dear {first name},` / `Hi {first name},` | `Best wishes,` / `Take care,` / `Love,` | Contractions, idioms, questions, warmth |

Pick the register once, from the greeting, and hold it for the whole letter.

### Four-paragraph skeleton

```
Para 1  Greeting + purpose sentence (I am writing to …) + one line of background
Para 2  Bullet point 1 — the issue/request + detail or reason
Para 3  Bullet point 2 (+ bullet point 3) — detail or reason
Para 4  What you want to happen next + closing sentence + sign-off
```

Bullet order may change, but every bullet needs detail — "Yes, I did that." is not coverage.

### Purpose sentences

```
I am writing to enquire about {course / service / accommodation}.
I am writing to express my dissatisfaction with {thing}.
I am writing to apply for the position of {job}.
I am writing to request {thing}.
I am writing to apologise for {action}.
I am writing to invite you to {event}.
I am writing to thank you for {help}.
I am writing with regard to {matter}.
I am writing in response to your advertisement in {source}.
I'm writing to let you know that {news}.            (informal)
```

### Function frames

**Complaint** — *Unfortunately, {problem} did not match the description in your advertisement.* / *To make matters worse, {further problem}.* / *I would therefore be grateful if you could {action}.* / *I look forward to hearing how you intend to resolve this matter.*

**Apology** — *I am extremely sorry that {event}.* / *The reason for this was that {cause}.* / *To make up for the inconvenience, I would like to {offer}.*

**Enquiry / request** — *Could you please let me know whether {information}?* / *I would be grateful if you could provide me with {information}.* / *I would also like to know if {condition}.*

**Suggestion** — *I would like to suggest that {idea}.* / *One possible solution would be to {plan}.* / *It might be a good idea to {action}.*

**Invitation (informal)** — *I was wondering if you'd like to {activity}.* / *Do let me know if {condition} — it would be lovely to see you.*

**Thanks** — *I really appreciate {thing} — it made a great difference to {person/situation}.*

**Closing sentences** — *I look forward to hearing from you.* (formal / semi-formal) · *Please do not hesitate to contact me if you need further information.* · *Thank you for your time and consideration.* (application) · *Drop me a line when you get a chance.* (informal)

### Critique order for letters

1. Purpose clear? 2. All bullets covered and extended? 3. Register right for the reader and consistent? 4. Is the communicative goal achieved (does the reader know what to do)? 5. Then CC, LR, GRA as for Academic. Score with the same four-line block as Step 4; use Step 5 for resubmissions.

Common letter mistakes: one-line bullets; register drift; long irrelevant backstory; memorised openers that don't fit the reader; no clear request or next step; wrong closing for the greeting (*Dear Sir or Madam … Yours sincerely*).

## Frequent Academic faults

| Fault | Why it costs marks | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Overview missing, or full of numbers | TA is held down hard without a real summary | Two general sentences, no figures |
| Prompt copied into the opening | Copied words are not counted as the learner's own language | Rebuild the sentence with new grammar |
| Every data point described | Shows no selection; usually weak CC too | Keep four to six features, group the rest |
| Figures misread or units wrong | Inaccuracy is a TA fault | Recheck every number against the visual |
| Opinions or invented reasons | Report format is broken | Describe only what the visual shows |
| Under 150 words | TA penalty | Count before submitting |

## Output format

**Writing a model report:** only with invented or learner-supplied data, never an official sample script. Mark the paragraphs (Opening, Overview, Body A, Body B), give the word count, and add two or three lines on why those features were chosen and which language choices matter.

**Giving feedback:** quote the learner's sentence, show the improved version, and say which criterion it affects. Finish with the score block from Step 4 and the one or two priorities for the next draft.
